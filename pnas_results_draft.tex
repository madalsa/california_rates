\section*{Results}

\subsection*{Revenue requirement and rate design levers.}

California's three investor-owned utilities have a combined authorized revenue requirement of \$42.1 billion. Residential customers fund \$17.5 billion of this total, with shares varying by utility: 41\% for PG\&E, 44\% for SCE, and 37\% for SDG\&E (Fig.~1). Transmission and distribution infrastructure dominates the cost structure, accounting for 56--57\% of residential revenue across all three utilities. Generation and energy procurement represent the second largest component at 26--29\%.

The three policy levers we examine target different slices of this revenue. Wildfire costs, accumulated from the catastrophic fire seasons of 2017--2020, represent the single largest reform opportunity. PG\&E carries \$5.4 billion in wildfire liabilities (27\% of its residential revenue), reflecting its exposure in high-fire-risk Northern California. SCE's wildfire burden is \$2.9 billion (17\%), and SDG\&E's is \$0.4 billion (10\%). The authorized return on equity generates \$2.2 billion annually for both PG\&E and SCE (earned on rate bases exceeding \$41 billion each) and \$0.7 billion for SDG\&E. A one percentage point reduction in ROE would reduce residential bills by 1.1\% for PG\&E, 1.2\% for SCE, and 1.7\% for SDG\&E. T\&D costs, at 56--57\% of residential revenue, define the upper bound of what fixed charges could collect if all T\&D costs were shifted from volumetric rates.

\subsection*{Revenue neutrality and scenario validation.}

We first verify that the benchmark designed rate (F0\_WF0\_ROE0) replicates actual tariff bills (Fig.~2a). The scaling factor equals 1.000 for all three utilities, confirming that designed volumetric rates exactly match the actual TOU rates. The mean per-building bill difference is less than \$0.10 for PG\&E and SDG\&E. Revenue-neutral scenarios (CPUC Fixed Charge and 50\% Fixed Charge) collect the same total revenue as the reference TOU rate. Cost-reduction scenarios track their target reductions precisely: wildfire removal lowers collected revenue by 27\% for PG\&E, 17\% for SCE, and 10\% for SDG\&E.

\subsection*{Non-adopters: the majority of households.}

Non-adopters account for 74\% of sample buildings (16,332 in PG\&E, 13,298 in SCE, 3,195 in SDG\&E). These households have no rooftop solar, no battery storage, and no electric vehicle. Their bills respond directly and predictably to rate structure changes.

Under the reference TOU rate, non-CARE households pay \$3,297--\$3,807/yr and CARE households pay \$1,536--\$1,759/yr, depending on utility (Fig.~3, top left). The CPUC's income-graduated fixed charge, which is already approved and adds \$6/month for CARE and \$24.15/month for non-CARE while lowering volumetric rates, reduces bills for most households: PG\&E CARE customers save \$161/yr (10\%), non-CARE save \$221/yr (6\%). SCE shows a similar pattern. SDG\&E is the exception: CARE customers see a slight increase of \$30/yr (2\%) because the fixed charge exceeds the volumetric rate reduction for low-consumption CARE households.

Our 50\% fixed charge scenario produces a sharper redistribution. CARE bills fall by \$212 (PG\&E), \$227 (SCE), and \$71 (SDG\&E). Non-CARE bills increase by \$50 for PG\&E and \$25 for SDG\&E, while SCE non-CARE bills decrease slightly by \$24 (Fig.~4, top row). The direction of the non-CARE impact depends on the utility's specific consumption distribution and CARE enrollment share.

Wildfire removal produces the largest bill reduction of any single lever. PG\&E customers save \$451/yr (CARE) and \$959/yr (non-CARE). SCE customers save \$265 and \$548, respectively. SDG\&E customers save \$176 and \$371. These reductions reflect the wildfire cost shares embedded in each utility's rates. A one percentage point ROE reduction saves \$18--\$63/yr across utilities and income groups, a modest but uniform reduction.

The combined scenario (50\% fixed charges, wildfire removal, and ROE reduction) delivers the deepest savings. PG\&E CARE customers save \$554/yr (33\% reduction), while non-CARE customers save \$977/yr (27\%). SCE CARE customers save \$418/yr (27\%) and non-CARE save \$603/yr (18\%). The percentage savings are larger for CARE customers because the reduced CARE fixed charge (\$6/month) replaces a larger share of their volumetric bill.

\subsection*{CARE revenue burden shifts under fixed charges.}

CARE customers currently contribute 12.6--14.9\% of total residential revenue (Fig.~2b). This share is lower than their population share (22--30\% of customers) because the CARE discount reduces their per-kWh rate by 32.5--37\%. Fixed charge scenarios shift the CARE revenue burden further downward. Under 50\% fixed charges, the CARE share drops to 11.0\% for SCE (from 12.6\%) and to 13.1\% for PG\&E (from 14.9\%). This occurs because CARE fixed charges are set at 24.8\% of non-CARE levels, while CARE volumetric rates are only 63--68\% of non-CARE rates. The fixed charge is a steeper discount than the volumetric discount.

Cost-removal scenarios (wildfire and ROE) preserve the CARE share at approximately its reference level. These levers reduce volumetric rates uniformly, maintaining the proportional relationship between CARE and non-CARE bills.

\subsection*{EV adopters pay more, but reform reduces the burden.}

Households that own an electric vehicle but have no rooftop solar (2,107 in PG\&E, 1,690 in SCE, 391 in SDG\&E) pay \$4,100--\$4,600/yr under the reference rate, approximately \$1,200--\$1,300 more than non-adopters (Fig.~3, top right). The additional load comes from Level 2 home charging at 7.2 kW, adding approximately 2,900 kWh/yr for the average driver (24 miles/day at 3 mi/kWh). Charging occurs off-peak (10 PM to approximately 11 PM for the average driver), but the additional consumption still increases bills substantially.

Rate reform benefits EV adopters in absolute terms but does not eliminate the consumption penalty. Wildfire removal saves EV households \$600--\$1,000/yr, and the combined scenario saves \$900--\$1,500/yr (Fig.~4, second row). Fixed charge scenarios provide smaller savings to EV households than to non-adopters because EV households consume more electricity and therefore benefit more from volumetric rate reductions than from fixed charge additions.

\subsection*{PV-plus-storage adopters achieve near-zero bills.}

Households with rooftop solar and battery storage (3,182 in PG\&E, 2,601 in SCE, 606 in SDG\&E) achieve dramatic bill reductions under the current rate structure (Fig.~3, bottom left). PV systems sized at 90\% of native demand (mean 4--5 kW) paired with LP-optimized 13.5 kWh batteries reduce bills from \$3,300--\$3,900/yr to \$160--\$384/yr, a 90--95\% reduction. CARE PV households in PG\&E achieve effectively zero bills (\$0/yr mean), while CARE PV households in SCE average just \$11/yr.

The mechanism is straightforward. These households import only 32--43\% of their native demand from the grid (2,800--3,800 kWh/yr vs 8,700--8,800 kWh native). The battery shifts solar generation from midday (when export compensation is low) to evening peak hours (when retail rates and export values are highest). Grid exports average 3,000--4,100 kWh/yr, generating \$900--\$2,100/yr in export compensation. The combination of reduced grid purchases and export revenue drives bills to near-zero.

Fixed charges fundamentally alter the economics of PV-plus-storage. Under 50\% fixed charges, the monthly fee (\$88--\$92/month for non-CARE) adds \$1,060--\$1,100/yr to the bill, compressing annual savings by \$800--\$870 compared to the reference rate (Fig.~4, third row). Non-CARE PV households in PG\&E see their bills rise from \$221/yr under the reference rate to \$1,109/yr under 50\% fixed charges. This increase exceeds any bill reduction from wildfire removal or ROE reduction. CARE PV households are partially shielded: their fixed charge is \$6/month (\$72/yr), and their bills rise from near-zero to approximately \$229--\$263/yr under 50\% fixed charges.

The bill increase for PV adopters under fixed charges represents the core policy tension. Under volumetric-only rates, PV-plus-storage households avoid paying for grid infrastructure by minimizing grid purchases. Fixed charges ensure a minimum contribution to grid costs regardless of self-generation. Whether this constitutes fair cost allocation or a penalty for clean energy investment depends on one's view of who should fund the grid.

\subsection*{Combined PV, EV, and storage households.}

Households with all three technologies (529 in PG\&E, 496 in SCE, 125 in SDG\&E) achieve 73--83\% bill reductions under the reference rate, with average bills of \$591--\$1,065/yr (Fig.~3, bottom right). The savings are smaller than PV-only households because EV charging adds 2,900 kWh/yr that solar and battery cannot fully offset. Self-sufficiency ratios average 58--69\%, compared to 62--77\% for PV-only households. Grid imports are higher (4,500--5,700 kWh/yr) and exports are lower (2,100--3,000 kWh/yr).

Under 50\% fixed charges, these households see bill increases of \$300--\$500/yr for non-CARE customers (Fig.~4, bottom row). The impact is smaller than for PV-only households because their higher grid imports generate more volumetric revenue, partially offsetting the fixed charge. CARE households with all three technologies see modest bill changes in either direction depending on the utility.

\subsection*{Climate zone variation within PG\&E.}

We examine how bill impacts vary across PG\&E's climate zones, grouped into coastal/mild (CEC zones 1--5), inland/moderate (zones 6, 12), and inland/hot (zones 11, 13--14, 16) (Fig.~5). Inland/hot zones have higher baseline consumption driven by air conditioning loads, resulting in larger absolute bill changes under all scenarios.

For non-adopters, wildfire removal saves inland/hot non-CARE households approximately \$1,200/yr compared to \$700/yr for coastal households. The 50\% fixed charge scenario shows a regressive pattern within climate zones: non-CARE households in inland/hot zones see the largest bill increases (\$200--\$400/yr) because their high consumption generates larger volumetric savings that are partially replaced by the fixed charge.

For PV-plus-storage adopters, the climate zone pattern reverses. Non-CARE households in all zones see bill increases under 50\% fixed charges, but inland/hot households have higher self-sufficiency (benefiting from greater solar resource) and higher baseline bills, making the relative impact smaller. The error bars in Fig.~5 indicate substantial within-zone variation, reflecting differences in building size, occupancy, and efficiency within each climate group.
